My path into product didn't run through design school. It ran through MySpace.

I was customizing profile pages with HTML and CSS, learning by breaking things and then working out how to fix them. That was the first time it landed that you could type something and change what another person saw. I never really stopped. Twenty years and more than 150 websites later, freelance work has run alongside every job I've held — it's the part of my career that never had a title.

For a long time I treated it as a side thing. A hobby that happened to pay. It took an embarrassingly long time to notice it was the best product training I was getting.

## Nobody wants a website

Here is the thing you learn in your first ten client projects and never unlearn: nobody actually wants a website.

They want bookings. They want to look credible enough that a stranger will wire them money. They want to stop answering the same email fifteen times a week. The website is the thing they've decided will get them there, and often they're wrong about that — the real problem is their pricing, or their intake form, or the fact that nobody can find their phone number.

A client says *"I need a new homepage."* What's usually true is *"people land on my site and leave without calling me."* Those are different problems with different solutions, and only one of them is a homepage.

Learning to hear the second thing when someone asks for the first is most of what product management turns out to be. The difference is that in a company, the misdiagnosis arrives with more authority attached. It comes as a roadmap item, or a competitor screenshot, or an executive who saw something at a conference. The instinct you need is the same — ask what would have to be true for this to be the right thing to build — but it's harder to exercise when the person who misdiagnosed it signs off on your review.

## You feel the whole system

Freelance work makes you responsible for everything, which is inefficient and enormously educational.

You scope it, price it, build it, deploy it, and then you're the one who gets the message eight months later saying it's broken. There's nobody to hand the consequences to. That closes a loop most product roles never close: you find out what your decisions actually cost, at the exact moment they cost it.

Somewhere past a hundred sites you stop seeing pages and start seeing systems. The same handful of problems keep appearing wearing different visual clothes — content that nobody will maintain, a form that asks for more than anyone will give, navigation that mirrors an org chart instead of what a visitor came for. Once you've seen a pattern break in twenty places, you recognise it in the wireframe.

That's the part that transfers. Not the CSS. The pattern library in your head.

## The degree wasn't the part that mattered

I studied computer science. Almost nothing I use daily came from that.

What I actually rely on came from volume. When you've deployed a few hundred things, you develop a decent instinct for which technical claims are load-bearing and which are decoration. You can tell when an estimate has a hidden dependency in it. You know roughly what "we'd need to refactor that first" costs, because you've been on the wrong side of that sentence yourself.

A degree gives you vocabulary, which is genuinely useful — you can follow the conversation. It doesn't give you judgment about which parts of the conversation matter.

This matters more as AI reshapes what teams do. When execution gets cheaper, the scarce skill isn't producing the artifact — it's judging whether the artifact is any good, and whether it was the right artifact. That judgment comes from having built things and watched them fail. There isn't a shortcut, and I don't think a certification produces it.

## The gap is the interesting part

What's stayed constant across freelance projects, marketplaces, fintech, payments, and enterprise platforms is the part I enjoy most: the space between a rough idea and a polished product.

It isn't about making things look good. It's about building systems that work, solving real problems for real people, and making software that feels less like software and more like a well-made tool.

I learned that on MySpace profile pages, which is a ridiculous sentence to write and completely true.
