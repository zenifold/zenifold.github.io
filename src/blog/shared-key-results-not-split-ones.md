Almost every company I've worked with could list twenty reasonable things to do next. Almost none could explain why the top three earned their place over the other seventeen.

That's what strategy is for, and it's why I keep coming back to OKRs despite how thoroughly the format has been discredited by bad implementations. The problem usually isn't the framework. It's one specific mistake that shows up in nearly every rollout.

## The failure isn't a bad plan, it's an unshared one

Leadership agrees on priorities in a room. Everyone leaves aligned. Three layers down, two teams are optimising for measures that quietly contradict each other.

Neither team is doing anything wrong. Both are hitting their numbers. Nobody connected their key results, so the conflict only becomes visible when something ships and doesn't work — which is the most expensive moment to discover it.

I've watched this play out enough times to think it's structural rather than a discipline problem. It comes from how the results get assigned.

## Split versus shared

When a key result spans two teams, there are two ways to handle it.

**Split it.** Platform owns their half, product owns theirs. Clean, legible, each team has a number they control. This is what most companies do, and it's the mistake.

**Share it.** Both teams own the same key result, at full value, and neither hits it unless the outcome happens.

The difference sounds like bookkeeping. It isn't. A key result owned by two teams gets *negotiated* — they have to talk, because neither can succeed alone. The same result split in half gets *optimised in two directions*, because each team can now hit their number in a way that makes the other team's harder.

Splitting converts a shared goal into two competing local ones and hides it behind a tidy spreadsheet.

## What that looked like

At a hyper-growth startup I architected a cross-functional OKR framework on this principle. Company objectives, then key results that deliberately crossed team boundaries and were jointly owned, then team commitments underneath.

The immediate effect wasn't better goal-setting. It was that conflicts surfaced weeks earlier, while they were still cheap to fix. Two teams who share a number talk in week two instead of week nine. Goal completion went up and the friction between teams went down, and I'd attribute most of that to the change in ownership rather than anything clever about the objectives themselves.

The uncomfortable part is that shared ownership feels bad to assign. It looks like accountability is being diluted. Someone will point out that you can't fire anyone over a number two teams own. That's true, and it's the wrong frame — the number exists to coordinate work, not to build a case against someone.

## Four things that make it hold

**Write outcomes you could be wrong about.** An objective nobody could fail is an objective nobody is steering by. If you'd hit it by doing nothing, it's a description of the status quo.

**Keep the cadence you'll actually keep.** A quarterly review that slips to twice a year is worse than a monthly one that holds, because the slipped version teaches everyone that the framework is theatre.

**Say what got cut.** A roadmap exists to make trade-offs legible. If it doesn't record what didn't make it and why, it's a wish list with dates.

**Let the cascade stop.** Not every team needs an OKR for every objective. Forcing full coverage produces invented goals, and invented goals are how the whole practice gets a bad name.

## The point isn't the framework

None of this is really about OKRs. You could run the same idea with any goal format.

The underlying claim is that misalignment is mostly a visibility problem, and that most operating systems are designed in a way that hides conflicts until they're expensive. Making dependencies explicit — putting two names on one number — is a cheap structural fix for something companies usually try to solve with more meetings.
