Ask an executive what threatens their business and you'll usually hear about competitors, hiring, or the funding environment. When we asked more than 4,000 of them to name what was actually slowing them down, the answer came back pointing inward.

**68% said disconnected systems and manual tasks were their number one threat.**

Not a rival. Not the market. The tooling they already own and pay for.

We've started calling this *operational drag*: the compounding cost of work that exists only because your systems don't talk to each other. It doesn't appear as a line item. Nobody is assigned to it. It shows up as everything taking slightly longer than it should, forever.

## What drag actually looks like

The pattern we see in engagements, which the research supports:

- **Re-entry.** The same information typed into two systems because nothing bridges them.
- **Reconciliation.** Time spent deciding which of two numbers is correct, rather than acting on either.
- **Chasing.** Work that stalls because a status lives in someone's head or inbox.
- **Workarounds that became process.** A spreadsheet built to patch a gap five years ago that is now load-bearing and undocumented.

Each of these is individually too small to escalate. That's precisely why they persist — no single instance is worth a meeting, and the aggregate never gets measured.

## The trap underneath it

The second finding was closely related: organisations describing themselves as **data-rich but insight-poor**. [DATA — the percentage, and how the question was phrased.]

These two findings are the same problem seen from different ends. Disconnected systems don't just cost time; they fragment the data. You end up with a great deal of information and no position from which to see it whole. Then the instinct is to buy an analytics tool, which produces a dashboard on top of fragmented data — faster access to a picture that's still incomplete.

## Why this is the AI question, not a separate one

Most AI programmes we're asked to review start in the wrong place: a customer-facing feature, chosen because it's visible and demos well.

Meanwhile the same company has [DATA — number, if you have it] hours a week going into moving information between systems by hand. That work is repetitive, rule-heavy, well-documented by the people doing it, and almost perfectly suited to automation. It's also unglamorous, which is exactly why it stays on the list.

The test I'd apply to any AI initiative: **does it move a number somebody already cared about before you arrived?** Operational drag passes that test trivially, because people have been complaining about it for years. A speculative customer-facing feature usually doesn't.

## What to do this quarter

Not a transformation programme. Three concrete things:

**1. Measure one drag path.** Pick a single workflow crossing two or more systems. Time it end to end, including waiting. Most organisations have never measured one, and the number is usually indefensible once it's written down.

**2. Find the load-bearing spreadsheet.** Every company has one — a file doing critical work that no system owns. It's a precise map of where your tooling doesn't meet.

**3. Automate the boring middle, not the ends.** The highest-return automation is rarely at the customer edge. It's the hand-off nobody owns between two teams that both assume the other has it.

## The uncomfortable version

Operational drag is under-addressed because it's nobody's job. It doesn't sit in one function, so it never gets an owner, a budget, or a slide. It's the tax you pay for having grown faster than you integrated — which nearly every successful company has.

The good news is that it's tractable in a way most strategic threats aren't. You can't out-execute a competitor by deciding to. You can absolutely remove a hand-off.
